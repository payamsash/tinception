### [0:20 — 1:50] Question one: what I'd add

Three things.

**First**, I work across methods that don't usually sit in one person — structural MRI across thirteen sites, resting-state EEG across seven, MEG, and genetics in the UK Biobank. Most people there know one of those better than I do. Few have had to make them all agree.

`[breath]`

**Second** — and this is the one I'd actually offer — my hands are on the data. So I see what doesn't survive into the summary.

An example. A colleague's manuscript claimed an EEG measure could work as a diagnostic test. I recomputed it: at the real prevalence of tinnitus, it would be wrong about three times out of four. But the effect itself was solid — it held when I dropped each site in turn.

So the finding was real. The claim about it was too strong. We changed the claim, not the data.

`[breath]`

**Third**, I've done this format before — Brainhack, and a health hackathon building assistive devices with the people who'd actually use them. What I took from those is that the useful person isn't the one with the best idea. It's the one who can get it working before everyone leaves.

That's the part I can do. I contribute to MNE-Python, and I wrote the EEG pipeline the TIDE consortium runs on — so when a question comes up, I can usually answer it before people go home.

`[breath]`

### [1:50 — 4:25] Question two: do biological subtypes exist?

My answer is **yes — but almost none of the subtypes we publish are among them.**

The distinctions that hold up have one thing in common: they were defined in the clinic first, and *then* tested for biology.

Three examples. **Pulsatile tinnitus** is genuinely a different condition — a vascular cause, sometimes curable. **Bilateral versus one-sided tinnitus** differ in how strongly they're inherited: in a study of ten thousand twin pairs, tinnitus in both ears was about twice as heritable. And **constant versus occasional tinnitus** differ on an objective brainstem measurement — occasional tinnitus looks like no tinnitus at all.

`[breath]`

**None of those came from clustering brain data.** Each started from an obvious clinical split, and then asked whether the biology followed.

When you cluster brain data instead, it doesn't hold. I've tried twice — structural MRI across thirteen sites, EEG across seven. Both times I found two groups. Both times, tested properly, they overlapped almost completely.  `[hold up SLIDE 1 — about 5s]` And in the UK Biobank, my own model split healthy people the same way it split patients. Whatever it found, it wasn't tinnitus.

`[breath]`

I'm not alone. The one large replication attempt in our field, nearly eighteen hundred patients, failed — and concluded tinnitus is a continuum, not clear subgroups. Psychiatry went through this with depression: re-analysed with the proper test, the clusters weren't there.

I did it to myself, too. The cleanest subtype result I ever produced turned out to be a single scanner.

`[breath]`

So what *is* real? In both my cohorts, the strongest thing separating patients is **hearing**. The proportion whose brains look abnormal rises steadily with hearing loss — in the MRI cohort, from eight percent to thirty-four.  `[hold up SLIDE 2 — about 5s]`  But it rises as a gradient, not a boundary. And distress predicts almost nothing.

What would change my mind: a subtype that predicts something the model never saw — treatment response, or how someone does over two years. Nobody has passed that test.

Why this matters to you: **enrich a trial on a subtype that isn't real, and you lose power — you can make a working drug look like a failure.**

`[breath]`

One last thought. If this really is a gradient rather than a set of boxes, we shouldn't wait for the boxes to be agreed before treating people.

My thesis system picks its target from each patient's own brain — not from a subtype label.

Thank you for considering me.

## Five follow-ups this video invites

**"So you don't believe in precision medicine for tinnitus?"**
The opposite. I think precision medicine is right and subtyping is one route to it — just not the route that's been tried. Stratifying on things we can already measure reliably, like laterality, constancy and hearing profile, is cheap and has genetic and physiological backing. Discovering categories by clustering brain scans doesn't, yet.

**"Isn't your negative result just a power problem?"**
Partly, and I'd say so. But two of my checks don't depend on sample size: the split appears just as strongly in healthy controls as in patients, and the two groups differ in *degree* along one direction rather than having different patterns. Neither of those gets fixed by more subjects.

**"The Nature Reviews Primer argues for enrichment strategies. Do you disagree?"**
No — I agree with it. My addition is just that enrichment is only as good as the stratifier. If you enrich on something real you gain power; if you enrich on something spurious you lose it twice over, and you may conclude a working drug doesn't work.

**"Isn't the hearing axis just hearing loss, not tinnitus?"**
That was my worry too, so we tested it. In the EEG cohort we matched patients exactly on high-frequency hearing, and the brain difference got *larger*, not smaller. So within tinnitus patients, it isn't reducible to the audiogram. What we haven't yet shown is that the same axis is absent in people without tinnitus — and that's the analysis I'd run first.

**"What would you actually want to do at the Hackathon?"**
Test other people's subtype hypotheses against two multi-site datasets that are already processed, and be honest about what comes back. I can turn a proposed stratifier into a result quickly, including the null-model test that most of this literature has skipped.
